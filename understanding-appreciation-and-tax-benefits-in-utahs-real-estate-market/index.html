Utah Real Estate Tax Benefits: Capital Gains, 1031, Depreciation

First-Time Buyers House Hacking Investing

Utah real estate tax benefits are the quietest part of a Wasatch Front investor's return, which is why they are also the most misunderstood. This guide walks the five tax levers that actually move the needle for Utah homeowners and investors heading into 2026: the Section 121 primary-residence capital gains exclusion, the 1031 exchange for investment property, the 27.5-year residential depreciation schedule, cost segregation to accelerate that depreciation, and Utah's primary-residential property tax exemption — including the 2027 change.

Up front: taxes are the one area where blog advice is most dangerous. Every situation has details that change the answer. Nothing here is tax advice — it is education. Before you act on any of this, consult a Utah-licensed CPA who runs the specific numbers against your tax return and your exit plan.

Section 121: The Utah Homeowner's Biggest Tax Benefit

If you own and live in your Utah home as your primary residence for at least 2 of the last 5 years before selling, the federal Section 121 exclusion lets you exclude up to $250,000 of capital gain from your income if filing single, or $500,000 if married filing jointly. On a Wasatch Front home bought in 2015 and sold in 2026, that is often the difference between a six-figure tax bill and zero federal tax on the gain.

What this means in practice:

  • You can use the exclusion more than once, but not more than once every two years.
  • The 2-of-5 rule does not have to be continuous. Any combination of 24 months in the last 60 counts.
  • Partial exclusions exist for job relocation, health reasons, or other qualifying events — talk with your CPA.
  • Any gain above the exclusion is federal long-term capital gains (0%, 15%, or 20% based on income) plus Utah state income tax.

For sellers sitting on big post-2020 Wasatch Front appreciation, this is often the single most valuable tax lever available. Time your sale around the 2-of-5 rule if you are close.

1031 Exchange: Deferring Tax on Utah Investment Property

Section 121 does not apply to investment property. Section 1031 does. A 1031 exchange lets you sell investment real estate and defer federal and (in most cases) Utah capital gains plus depreciation recapture by reinvesting the proceeds into like-kind replacement property.

The mechanics are strict:

  • 45-day identification window. You have 45 days from the closing of the relinquished property to identify replacement property in writing.
  • 180-day close window. You must close on the replacement property within 180 days of selling the original.
  • Qualified intermediary required. You cannot touch the sale proceeds. A qualified intermediary holds them.
  • Like-kind, equal-or-greater value. The replacement must be investment or business real estate of equal or greater value, with all equity reinvested, to fully defer.
  • No personal residence. A primary home does not qualify as either side of a 1031.

Utah investors use 1031 exchanges most often to ladder from a single rental into a small multifamily, or out of a hands-on Wasatch Front duplex into a passive DST. Depreciation: The Paper-Loss Shield on Utah Rentals

Residential rental property depreciates over 27.5 years for federal tax purposes. You take the building's cost basis — purchase price minus the land value, which is not depreciable — divide by 27.5, and deduct that amount from rental income each year. It is a paper loss; the property is almost certainly appreciating in dollars while the IRS lets you deduct it on a straight-line schedule.

Worked example. You buy a Salt Lake County duplex for $500,000. The county assessor allocates $100,000 to land and $400,000 to the building. Your annual depreciation deduction is $400,000 / 27.5 = $14,545 per year. That deduction offsets rental income first and can — subject to passive-loss rules — sometimes offset other income for qualifying real estate professionals.

Key caveats:

  • Depreciation recapture. When you sell, the IRS "recaptures" accumulated depreciation at up to 25% federal. A 1031 exchange defers this.
  • Passive activity loss rules. Depreciation losses from rentals are generally passive and only offset passive income — unless you qualify as a real estate professional for tax purposes. Rules are specific; CPA territory.
  • Allocation matters. The split between land (non-depreciable) and building (depreciable) materially affects your deduction. Get it right from day one.

Cost Segregation: Accelerating Utah Rental Depreciation

Standard 27.5-year depreciation treats the whole building as one asset. A cost segregation study breaks the building into components with shorter useful lives — carpet, appliances, cabinetry, driveways, landscaping — that depreciate over 5, 7, or 15 years instead of 27.5.

On a Wasatch Front rental or small commercial property, a cost segregation study can accelerate 20-30% of the building's basis into the first several years, producing much larger early-year paper losses. For high-income investors or those actively buying, this is often the difference between a mediocre after-tax return and a strong one. The study itself typically costs $3,000-$10,000; it rarely pencils on a single sub-$400k rental, but on a small multifamily or a portfolio it almost always does.

See our cost segregation service page for Utah-specific examples and talk with your CPA about whether your property is a candidate.

Utah's Primary Residential Exemption and the 2027 Change

Utah property tax is based on assessed value, but owner-occupied primary residences receive a 45% exemption — meaning you pay tax on 55% of the market value, not 100%. Investor-owned rentals and second homes do not qualify.

A statutory change scheduled for 2027 affects how the primary-residential exemption interacts with other factors. The practical impact varies by situation: some homeowners see a modest shift in effective rate, while others on the margin — owners who have converted a primary residence to a rental, or vice versa, during a cycle — see a bigger change. For the full breakdown, read our 2027 Utah primary-residential-exemption article and talk with your county assessor's office and a Utah-licensed CPA about your specific property.

For investors: this exemption is a reason to be careful about converting a rental back to a primary residence or vice versa. Document move-in and move-out dates meticulously; your county assessor will ask.

A Note on Opportunity Zones

Federal Opportunity Zone tax benefits have evolved since the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, and Utah has a number of designated zones in Salt Lake, Utah, Weber, and Cache counties. The tax deferral available depends on when you invest and when you realize underlying gain — rules have changed materially since 2019. Treat any specific Opportunity Zone claim you read online with skepticism, and confirm the current version with your CPA.

Putting the Utah Tax Playbook Together

A Wasatch Front investor building wealth over a 20-year horizon typically stacks these tax benefits in sequence: Section 121 on a primary residence sold every 5-10 years to free down-payment capital, depreciation on each rental held in the portfolio, cost segregation on larger or portfolio-level acquisitions, and 1031 exchanges to ladder up without paying tax at each step. Done well, this materially compresses the effective tax drag on a Utah real estate wealth-building plan.

Done poorly — a 1031 window missed by a day, depreciation miscalculated, land-building allocation off — any one of these mistakes can cost more than the deduction was worth. Consult a Utah-licensed CPA before you execute any of this. Frequently Asked Questions

What are the tax benefits of owning real estate in Utah?

Utah property owners can tap several federal tax benefits plus one Utah-specific one. Federal: the Section 121 primary-residence capital gains exclusion ($250k single / $500k married), mortgage interest and property tax itemized deductions subject to current caps, Section 1031 exchange for investment property, and the 27.5-year depreciation schedule on residential rentals. Utah-specific: the primary-residential exemption, which reduces the taxable value of an owner-occupied home by 45% today — with a change scheduled for 2027 that affects how that exemption applies. Investment-owned rentals don't get the primary exemption. Always consult a Utah-licensed CPA before applying any of these to your situation.

Do I have to pay capital gains tax when selling my Utah home?

Not necessarily. If the home was your primary residence for at least 2 of the last 5 years, Section 121 lets you exclude up to $250,000 of gain if single or $500,000 if married filing jointly. You can use this exclusion repeatedly, but not more than once every two years. Above those thresholds, long-term capital gains tax applies at the federal rate (0%, 15%, or 20% depending on income) plus Utah's state income tax rate on the excess. If the home was a rental, Section 121 doesn't apply unless you meet the 2-of-5 primary-residence test, and recaptured depreciation is taxed separately.

How does a 1031 exchange work for Utah investors?

A Section 1031 exchange lets you defer federal (and in most cases Utah) capital gains and depreciation recapture when you sell investment real estate and reinvest the proceeds in like-kind property. The rules are strict: you have 45 days from closing on the sale to identify replacement property, 180 days to close on it, and you must use a qualified intermediary (not touch the funds yourself). The replacement must be investment or business real estate, equal or greater in value, and you must reinvest all equity to fully defer. See our 1031 exchange page for Utah mechanics and consult a Utah-licensed CPA for your specific transaction.

How does depreciation work on a Utah rental property?

Residential rental property depreciates over 27.5 years for federal tax purposes. You take the building's cost basis (land is not depreciable), divide by 27.5, and deduct that amount from rental income each year. On a $400,000 rental in Utah with land valued at $80,000, that's roughly $11,600 per year in paper-loss depreciation. Cost segregation studies can accelerate a portion of that into the first few years. When you sell, the IRS "recaptures" depreciation at up to 25% federal — a 1031 exchange defers this. A Utah-licensed CPA should run the actual numbers for your property.

What is the Utah primary residential exemption?

The primary residential exemption reduces the taxable value of an owner-occupied Utah home by 45% for property tax purposes, meaning you pay property tax on 55% of the market value instead of 100%. Investment-owned rentals and second homes don't qualify. A statutory change scheduled for 2027 changes how the exemption interacts with other factors — see our dedicated primary-residential-exemption article for the full detail and talk with your county assessor or a Utah-licensed CPA about your specific property.
