Radon Testing When Buying a Utah Home: Timing, Cost, and Negotiating Mitigation

Buying Home Prep

Most Utah buyers walk into due diligence with a mental checklist: home inspection, appraisal, maybe a sewer scope. Radon often doesn't make the list. That's a miss, because it's one of the few problems you can't see, smell, or spot on a walkthrough, and it's common here.

According to the Utah Department of Environmental Quality, about one in three Utah homes tests above the EPA's recommended action level. The good news: testing is cheap, mitigation is a well-understood fix, and the due diligence period is the best time to deal with both.

This guide covers when to schedule a radon test during your purchase, which kind of test to use, what mitigation typically involves and costs, and how to bring it up with the seller if the number comes back high.

This article is general information, not legal, health, or inspection advice. Radon guidance here comes from Utah DEQ and the EPA. For questions about your purchase contract, talk with your agent or a Utah-licensed real estate attorney; for health questions, start with Utah DEQ's radon program or your doctor. Mitigation costs vary by home, so get bids from certified contractors.

What Radon Is and Why It Matters in Utah

Radon is a naturally occurring radioactive gas. It forms when uranium in soil and rock breaks down, then moves up through the ground and into homes through cracks in the foundation, gaps around pipes, and sump pits. Once it's inside, it can build up, especially in basements and lower levels.

Utah's geology is part of the story. A lot of the state's homes sit on soils that release radon, and a lot of Wasatch Front homes have basements, which is exactly where radon tends to collect. DEQ reports that Utah homes average around 5.3 picocuries per liter (pCi/L) of air, which is above the federal action level.

Here's the number that matters for buyers:

4.0 pCi/L is the level at which the EPA recommends fixing a home. The EPA also suggests considering mitigation for results between 2 and 4 pCi/L, since lower is better.

We'll leave the health side to the experts. Utah DEQ and the EPA both treat radon as a long-term exposure issue worth testing for, and that's all you need to know to justify adding a test to your purchase. For health questions, DEQ's radon program and your doctor are the right sources.

One more thing to know up front: you can't predict radon by neighborhood. Two houses next door to each other can test very differently. New construction, older homes, homes with and without basements: the only way to know is to test the specific house.

When to Test: Build Radon Into Your Due Diligence Timeline

On a Utah purchase contract, the due diligence period is your window to investigate the property and decide whether to move forward, ask the seller for changes, or walk away. Every inspection you want, radon included, has to fit inside that window. Your agent will flag the exact deadlines in your contract, but the practical takeaway is simple: schedule the radon test at the same time as your home inspection, not after.

Here's why timing matters so much:

  • The test itself takes days. A short-term test runs for roughly two to four days, and the house generally needs to be closed up (windows and doors shut, except for normal coming and going) for about 12 hours before it starts.
  • Results take time to come back. Depending on the test type, you may have results immediately or wait on lab processing.
  • You need room to react. If the result is high, you want time to get a mitigation bid and negotiate with the seller before your deadline, not the night before.

Many home inspectors in Utah offer radon testing as an add-on, which makes scheduling easy: one appointment, one set of results. If yours doesn't, book a separate radon tester for the same week.

Test where someone could actually live

The EPA's home buyer and seller guidance recommends testing the lowest level of the home that could be used regularly. That matters if the seller used the basement for storage, but you plan to turn it into a family room, an office, or a basement apartment for house hacking. Test for how you'll use the home, not how the seller did.

Short-Term Tests vs. Continuous Monitors

There are a few ways to test, and the right one for a purchase is usually not the same one you'd use in a home you already own.

Passive short-term kits

These are the small canisters you can buy at a hardware store or order from DEQ (the state sells a low-cost kit to Utah residents, which was around $11 including lab processing at last check). You set it out, leave it for a few days, and mail it to a lab.

Good for: testing a home you already own, or a quick screen.
The catch in a purchase: mailing and lab turnaround can eat a week or more of your due diligence period, and there's no record of what happened in the house during the test.

Continuous radon monitors (CRMs)

This is what most professional testers use in real estate transactions. The device records radon levels hour by hour, gives a result as soon as the test ends, and often includes tamper detection that flags if the device was moved or if conditions changed, like windows being opened.

Good for: purchases. You get fast results, a written report you can hand to the seller, and a record that holds up better in a negotiation.

Long-term tests

Long-term tests run for more than 90 days and give a better picture of average levels across seasons. They're a great idea once you own the home, but they don't fit inside a typical due diligence window.

A good rule of thumb: use a certified tester with a continuous monitor during the purchase, then run a long-term test sometime in your first year of ownership, especially through a winter, when homes are closed up more.

Finding a qualified tester

Utah DEQ points homeowners to two national certification programs: the National Radon Proficiency Program (NRPP) and the National Radon Safety Board (NRSB). Both have searchable directories of certified testers and mitigators in Utah. Ask your inspector which certification they hold before you book.

How to Read the Results

Your report will give you a number in pCi/L. Here's how to think about it as a buyer:

  • Below 2 pCi/L: No action needed. Keep the report with your closing documents and plan to retest every couple of years, as DEQ recommends.
  • 2 to 3.9 pCi/L: Below the action level, but in the range where the EPA suggests considering mitigation. Some buyers ask for a credit here; many don't. It's a judgment call, and it matters more if you'll be spending time in the basement.
  • 4.0 pCi/L or higher: At or above the EPA action level. This is where most buyers ask the seller to address it.

If a short-term result is very high, more than double the action level, DEQ recommends a second short-term test right away to confirm. With a professional continuous monitor during a purchase, the hour-by-hour data usually gives you enough confidence to move to the mitigation conversation.

What Radon Mitigation Involves and What It Costs

The most common fix is an active soil depressurization system. A certified mitigator drills a hole through the basement slab (DEQ describes it as about five inches across), runs PVC pipe from below the foundation up and out of the house, and installs a fan that pulls radon from the soil and vents it outside above the roofline. It's the PVC pipe with a small fan you've probably seen on the side of houses around the valley.

Installation usually takes a day. After the system has been running for at least 24 hours, the mitigator retests to confirm levels came down.

Typical cost range

Utah's public health data puts a typical mitigation system between about $1,000 and $2,500, and DEQ's own estimates fall in that range. Where a specific home lands depends on things like:

  • Whether the home has a basement, a slab, a crawl space, or a mix
  • The size of the foundation and how many sections it has
  • How the pipe has to be routed (through a garage and attic vs. up an exterior wall)
  • Whether a crawl space needs a vapor barrier

The only way to get a real number is a bid on the actual house. DEQ suggests getting three bids from certified contractors and making sure the contract states the radon level the mitigator will get the home down to.

Ongoing costs after you move in

The fan runs continuously. DEQ estimates the added electricity at roughly $8 to $10 a month, and the fan usually needs replacing after about 15 years. Every system should have a manometer, a small U-shaped gauge on the pipe that shows the fan is working. Glance at it every so often, and retest every two years or after any major remodel.

Negotiating Mitigation With the Seller

A high radon result doesn't have to derail a deal. It's one of the more straightforward inspection items to resolve, because the fix is well-defined and the cost is predictable. The EPA's guidance is that when a result comes back at or above 4 pCi/L, the buyer and seller should work out the cost and timing of mitigation. Who pays is a negotiation, not a rule.

Your options usually look like one of these:

1. The seller installs a system before closing

Clean and simple, as long as you protect yourself on quality. If you go this route, ask for:

  • A certified mitigator (NRPP or NRSB)
  • A copy of the contract and any warranty
  • A post-mitigation test result before closing, showing levels below 4 pCi/L

2. A seller credit or price reduction

Many buyers prefer this. You get a bid from a mitigator you choose, ask for a credit in that amount, and have the system installed after closing. You control who does the work and how the pipe is routed, which matters if you care about how it looks from the street or plan to finish the basement.

3. Split the cost

Splitting a mitigation bid is a reasonable middle ground, especially when the result is just over the action level.

Whatever you agree to, put it in writing as part of your due diligence response, before your deadline passes. A verbal "we'll take care of it" after the deadline isn't something you can count on.

If the home already has a system

Don't skip the test. Ask the seller for any past test results and the installer's information, check that the manometer shows the fan is running, and still run your own test. Systems fail, fans wear out, and remodels can change how well a system works.

If You're Selling: Test Before You List

If you're getting ready to sell your Utah home, a radon test is one of the cheapest pre-listing steps you can take. Testing a few weeks before you list lets you:

  • Avoid surprises mid-contract. A high result during the buyer's due diligence puts you on the clock. Finding out early lets you get bids and decide calmly.
  • Control the fix. If you mitigate before listing, you choose the contractor, get the post-mitigation test, and can hand buyers a clean report.
  • Answer disclosure questions honestly. If you've tested before, or have a system, share the results and paperwork. Talk with your agent about what your seller disclosures should include.

Keep in mind that buyers will likely test anyway, and their tester may place the device in a different spot. A recent professional test and a working, documented system go a long way toward a smooth due diligence period.

Your Next Step

If you're buying, add radon to your inspection plan the day your offer is accepted, ideally with the same inspector, using a continuous monitor, on the lowest level you'd actually use. If the number comes back at or above 4 pCi/L, get a mitigation bid and use it to negotiate a fix, a credit, or a split before your due diligence deadline.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I test for radon during a Utah home purchase?

Order the radon test on day one of your inspection period, bundled with your general home inspection. Utah's standard REPC due diligence window is short — usually 7 to 14 days — and a short-term radon test needs at least 48 hours of closed-house conditions before the device even reads. Starting late is the single most common reason buyers miss their chance to negotiate a mitigation.

How much does radon mitigation cost in Utah?

A typical active sub-slab depressurization system in Utah runs about $1,500 to $2,500 installed on a standard single-family home with an accessible basement. Complex foundations (multiple slabs, crawlspaces, finished basements with no clean exterior wall route) can push that into the $3,000 to $4,500 range. Get at least two licensed mitigator bids before you negotiate.

Who pays for radon mitigation, the buyer or the seller?

It's negotiable, and in Utah the outcome usually depends on how competitive the home is. In a balanced market, sellers typically install the system before closing or credit the buyer the cost at closing. In a seller's market on a desirable home, buyers more often absorb the cost themselves. Either way, raise it as a repair request inside your due diligence period — never after.

How do I negotiate radon mitigation with the seller?

Lead with the EPA guidance (action level 4.0 pCi/L), attach a licensed mitigator's written bid, and ask in writing for one of three things: the seller installs the system before closing through a licensed Utah mitigator, the seller credits you the bid amount at closing, or you split the cost. Keep the ask factual and documented — it's a health issue with a clear published standard, not a cosmetic request.

Should I retest after radon mitigation?

Yes. A post-mitigation test is the only way to confirm the system actually dropped the level below 4.0 pCi/L. Most reputable Utah mitigators include a post-install test or let you run your own continuous monitor for 48 to 96 hours before closing. Keep the test results and the installation paperwork — future buyers will ask for both.
