House Hack #2: How to Move Out of Your First Utah Deal and Buy the Next One

House Hacking Real Estate Investing Financing Scaling

Almost everything written about house hacking — including a fair amount of it on this site — is about getting the first one. Find the duplex. Get the 3.5% down FHA loan. Move in, rent the other side, cut your housing payment to almost nothing.

Then about a year goes by, and the question changes completely.

Now you're sitting on a property with a great loan on it, a tenant who pays like clockwork, and a growing sense that you could do this again. But nobody told you what happens next. Are you allowed to move out? Does the bank care? Do you lose the rate? Can you get another low-down-payment loan, or was that a one-time trick?

This is the article about deal #2. It's the part of the strategy where house hacking stops being a clever way to cut your rent and starts being an actual portfolio — and it's where most people stall out, not because the math stops working, but because nobody handed them the rules.

First Question: How Long Do You Actually Have to Live There?

This is the one everybody gets wrong, usually in the direction of being more scared than they need to be.

If you bought with an FHA loan, you agreed to occupy the home as your principal residence for at least one year after closing. That's the requirement. Not five years, not "until the loan is paid off" — one year.

VA loans carry a similar 12-month occupancy expectation. Conventional owner-occupied financing generally expects you to move in within 60 days of closing and to live there roughly a year.

So the practical answer for the overwhelming majority of Utah house hackers is: twelve months, and then you're free.

One serious caveat, because it matters: this only works if you genuinely intended to live there when you signed. Signing an occupancy affidavit while planning to move out in month two is mortgage occupancy fraud, and it's a federal crime — not a technicality. Live there, mean it, and the one-year mark arrives on its own.

Rule #1: Don't Sell It. Don't Refinance It Either.

When you move out, nothing happens to your mortgage. Your lender does not call the loan due. Your rate does not change. There's no clause that converts your terms the moment a tenant moves into your old bedroom.

Internalize what that means. If you locked a low rate, that loan is one of the most valuable assets you own — arguably more valuable than the equity sitting in the property. You cannot buy it again. Nobody will ever re-issue it to you. Every month that loan stays in place on a property that now collects full market rent, it quietly does work for you that no new deal can replicate.

Which is why the instinct to refinance and pull cash out for the down payment on deal #2 deserves real scrutiny. Sometimes it's right. But trading a 3% note for a 7% note to free up $40,000 is a very expensive way to raise a down payment, and a lot of people do it without ever running that comparison. Look at a HELOC — which sits behind your first mortgage and leaves it untouched — before you refinance the whole thing away.

Can You Just Get Another FHA Loan?

Usually not — and this is the wall most repeat house hackers hit first.

FHA generally limits you to one FHA-insured mortgage at a time. The program exists to help people buy a home to live in, not to finance a rental portfolio, so HUD doesn't hand out 3.5%-down loans indefinitely.

There are documented exceptions, and they're worth knowing because some Utah buyers legitimately qualify:

  • Relocation for work. If you're relocating for employment to an area outside reasonable commuting distance of your current home, you may be able to get a second FHA loan — and you don't have to sell the first house. (Think Salt Lake to St. George, not Sandy to Draper.)
  • Increase in family size. If your family has grown and the current home no longer meets your needs, a second FHA loan may be possible — but you'll typically need at least 25% equity in the existing home, verified by a current appraisal.
  • Vacating a jointly-owned property. Most commonly a divorce, where you move out and a co-borrower stays in the home.
  • Co-signing situations. If you're a non-occupying co-borrower on someone else's FHA loan, you may still be able to get your own.

These are real, but they're narrow. "I want to buy another duplex" is not on the list. So for most people the second deal runs through a different door entirely — and honestly, a better one.

The Move Most Utah House Hackers Should Make: 5% Down Conventional

Here's the rule change that should be far more famous than it is.

It used to be that buying a two-to-four unit property with conventional financing meant 15% down on a duplex and 25% down on a triplex or fourplex — numbers that put repeat house hacking out of reach for normal people. Fannie Mae changed that in late 2023. Owner-occupied two-to-four unit properties now go as low as 5% down.

Sit with that for a second. On a $700,000 Utah duplex, the difference between 15% down and 5% down is $70,000 of cash you no longer need to have. That single policy change is what makes stacking house hacks realistic for someone on a normal salary.

And because this is conventional financing rather than FHA, the one-loan-at-a-time problem disappears. You can keep your FHA loan on house hack #1 and go buy house hack #2 with a conventional owner-occupied loan. The only requirement is the familiar one: you have to actually move in and live there.

Single-family primary (conventional): 3%–5% down
Two-to-four unit, owner-occupied (conventional): as low as 5% down
Two-to-four unit, FHA: 3.5% down — but generally one FHA loan at a time
Eligible veterans (VA): potentially 0% down with remaining entitlement
Non-owner-occupied investment property: typically 20%–25% down

Look at that last line, then look at the ones above it. The entire financial advantage of house hacking is that you keep qualifying as an owner-occupant. That's the whole game. Every time you move, you get another shot at a down payment that an investor buying the identical building simply cannot get.

If you're a veteran, read this part twice

Utah has a large veteran population and VA entitlement is routinely left on the table. You are not necessarily limited to one VA loan at a time — remaining or "second-tier" entitlement can let eligible veterans finance another owner-occupied property, often with nothing down, while keeping the first one as a rental. If you served, ask a VA-savvy lender to calculate your remaining entitlement before you assume you've used it up.

How Lenders Treat the Rent From House Hack #1

The good news: the rent your old property now generates can help you qualify for the next loan. The less good news: not dollar for dollar.

Lenders typically count about 75% of market or lease rent, holding back roughly 25% as a vacancy and maintenance factor. Collect $2,400 a month across both sides of your old duplex and the underwriter is likely to credit you with about $1,800.

To get that credit at all, be ready with:

  • Signed leases for every unit, including the one you're vacating. Have the new tenant's lease executed before you apply, not after.
  • Proof of deposits — some lenders want to see the security deposit or first month's rent actually hit your account.
  • Cash reserves. Once you own multiple properties, expect to document several months of mortgage payments sitting in reserve. This requirement surprises people and it's the most common reason a pre-approval comes back smaller than expected.

One timing note that's easy to miss: a property you're converting from a primary residence to a rental sometimes gets treated more conservatively than a property you've rented for years. Tell your lender your plan up front so they can structure around it instead of discovering it three days before closing.

The Real Ceiling Isn't the Down Payment — It's DTI

Most people assume the limit on scaling is cash. It usually isn't. It's debt-to-income ratio.

Every mortgage you hold counts against your DTI in full. Rental income offsets it, but only at that 75% haircut, and only once it's documented. So the math that determines whether you can buy house hack #3 looks roughly like this: does 75% of your collected rents cover enough of your existing payments to leave room for a new one?

This is why the quality of your first deal matters so much more than the fact that you did one. A house hack that barely breaks even gives you almost nothing to work with when you go for the next loan. A house hack where the rents genuinely cover the payment with margin keeps your DTI healthy and keeps the door open.

If you're still shopping for deal #1, this is the argument for being patient on the numbers — and it's worth reading alongside how to analyze cash flow and ROI before you write an offer.

Three Pieces of Paperwork Everyone Forgets

These are unglamorous and they cause real damage when skipped.

1. Your insurance policy is now wrong

A homeowner's policy covers an owner-occupied home. The day your old unit becomes a pure rental, you need a landlord policy. Keep the wrong policy and you may discover at the worst possible moment — after a fire, a burst pipe, a liability claim — that your carrier can deny the claim because the property wasn't being used the way the policy described. Call your agent before the tenant moves in, not after. It's a ten-minute phone call.

2. Your HOA may cap rentals

A great many Utah townhome and condo communities limit the percentage of units that can be rented at any one time. If the cap is already met, you go on a waiting list — and your entire plan to move out and rent the place stalls indefinitely. Read the CC&Rs before you buy, not before you move. This single issue has wrecked more Utah house-hack exits than bad tenants ever have.

3. Your city may require a rental license

A number of Utah cities require landlords to hold a rental business license, and some pair it with an inspection or a "good landlord" training program that reduces your fee. Requirements vary meaningfully from city to city, so confirm with your municipality directly. It's typically inexpensive and quick — but operating without one can mean fines, and it's a bad surprise to hit while you're mid-purchase on the next property.

The Tax Clock Nobody Mentions

Two things change the moment your house hack becomes a full rental, and both have deadlines attached.

You start depreciating the whole property. While you lived there, you could generally only depreciate the rented portion. Once it's 100% a rental, the full structure goes on the schedule — a meaningful annual deduction. The flip side is depreciation recapture when you eventually sell, so it's a benefit with a bill attached at the end.

And the capital gains clock starts running. This is the one that costs people real money. The Section 121 exclusion lets you exclude a large amount of gain on the sale of a home — up to $250,000 if you're single, $500,000 if married filing jointly — but only if you lived in it as your primary residence for two of the previous five years.

Do the arithmetic: once you move out, you have roughly a three-year window to sell and still claim that exclusion. Given how much Utah property has appreciated, that exclusion can be worth tens of thousands of dollars. It is not something to stumble past by accident.

That doesn't mean you should sell — the long-term hold is usually the better play, and a 1031 exchange can defer gains if you do decide to move on. It means the decision should be made, with a CPA, inside that window rather than discovered after it closes. Talk to a tax professional before your three years are up.

What Stacking Actually Looks Like

Here's a realistic Wasatch Front sequence. Not a get-rich-quick timeline — just what the mechanics permit:

  • Year 0. Buy a duplex with 3.5% down FHA. Live in one side, rent the other. Housing cost drops to a few hundred dollars a month.
  • Year 1. Occupancy requirement satisfied. Sign a lease on your side, switch to a landlord policy, confirm the city's licensing rules. Now the property is fully rented and the FHA loan stays exactly as it is.
  • Year 1, same month. Buy property #2 as an owner-occupant with 5% down conventional. Move in. Rent what you're not using. Two properties, two low-down-payment loans, one of them collecting full rent.
  • Year 2. Repeat — if your DTI and reserves support it. This is where deal quality compounds or compounds against you.
  • Year 5+. Several properties, each bought at owner-occupant terms that an investor competing for the same buildings could never have gotten.

You are not required to move every twelve months, and plenty of people shouldn't. But the structural point stands: the owner-occupant discount is the single biggest edge available to a small investor, and it renews every time you move.

What Stalls Deal #2

  • Refinancing away a low rate to raise a down payment, without comparing it to a HELOC or second position loan first.
  • Letting DTI creep — financing a truck, taking on a big card balance, or co-signing something in the months before you apply. Underwriters see all of it.
  • No signed lease when you apply. Without it, the rent from your old unit may not count, and your approval shrinks accordingly.
  • Thin reserves. You scraped together the down payment and nothing else. Multi-property borrowers get asked for cushion.
  • Discovering the HOA rental cap after closing. Entirely preventable by reading the CC&Rs.
  • Treating the move-out as an afterthought. Tenant screening, the lease, insurance, and licensing all deserve the same care you gave the purchase.
  • Moving out early and calling it a technicality. It isn't one.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do I have to live in my house hack before I can move out?

FHA requires you to occupy the property as your principal residence for at least one year after closing. VA carries a similar 12-month expectation, and conventional owner-occupied loans generally expect you to move in within 60 days and stay about a year. After that occupancy period, you can typically move out and keep the loan in place.

Will my lender raise my rate or call the loan due when I move out?

No. Once you've satisfied the occupancy requirement, your mortgage continues on exactly the same terms. Lenders don't call owner-occupied loans due because the property later became a rental. If your rate is low, keeping that loan untouched is usually the most valuable thing you can do.

Can I have two FHA loans at the same time in Utah?

Generally no, unless you meet a specific exception — relocating for work beyond reasonable commuting distance, a documented increase in family size (which usually requires at least 25% equity in your current home), or vacating a jointly-owned property such as in a divorce. Most repeat house hackers simply use conventional financing for the second purchase instead.

How much do I need down on a second house hack?

Possibly far less than you think. Conventional financing now allows as little as 5% down on owner-occupied two-to-four unit properties, and 3%–5% on a single-family primary residence. Eligible veterans may be able to use remaining VA entitlement with no money down. Compare any of those to the 20%–25% an investor would need on the same building.

Does the rent from my first property help me qualify for the next one?

Usually, but at a discount. Lenders typically count about 75% of market or lease rent, holding back the rest for vacancy and maintenance. You'll generally need signed leases in hand before applying, and you should expect to document cash reserves as well.

What do I need to change when the property becomes a rental?

Switch your homeowner's policy to a landlord policy, confirm your HOA doesn't cap the number of rented units, and check whether your city requires a rental business license. All three are quick. All three are expensive to skip.

Should I sell my first house hack instead of renting it?

Usually not — but if you're going to, know that the Section 121 capital gains exclusion requires you to have lived in the home two of the previous five years, which gives you roughly a three-year window after moving out. Given Utah's appreciation, that exclusion can be substantial. Make that call deliberately with a CPA rather than letting the window close on its own.

Ready to Line Up Deal #2?

The first house hack is the hard one. The second is mostly a question of knowing the rules — which loan to use, what to keep, what to document, and what to check before you sign anything. None of it is complicated once someone lays it out. All of it is expensive to learn by trial and error.
