Utah Real Estate Laws: 2026 REPC, Disclosures, Earnest Money

Buying a Home First-Time Buyers Investing

Utah real estate laws are the quiet infrastructure under every Wasatch Front transaction. Most first-time buyers and new investors never read them — they just sign the REPC their REALTOR hands them and hope for the best. This guide walks the parts of Utah real estate law you actually need to understand to buy or invest well in 2026: the UAR REPC form, mandatory disclosures, earnest money mechanics, agency disclosure, and the handful of statutes that come up in practice.

Up front: this is educational, not legal advice. Utah law changes, and every transaction has specifics that matter. For a question that affects whether you buy, sell, or sue, consult a Utah-licensed real estate attorney. Your REALTOR is trained to walk the standard forms; they cannot give legal advice.

The Utah REPC: The Standard Contract Explained

The Real Estate Purchase Contract (REPC) is the standard Utah buy-sell agreement promulgated by the Utah Association of REALTORS. Virtually every Utah REALTOR uses this form on a residential purchase. Key sections to understand before you write or accept an offer:

  • Section 1 (property): the address, included items, and excluded items. Read the included-items list carefully — it determines whether the refrigerator, washer, and window coverings come with the house.
  • Section 2 (purchase price and financing): total price, financing details, and what loan type. Your REALTOR writes this in consultation with your lender.
  • Section 3 (earnest money): the up-front deposit (typically 1-3% of price) held by the title company or brokerage trust account until closing.
  • Section 7 (due diligence): the buyer's inspection window. Typically 7-21 days from contract. This is the main opportunity to inspect, test, and either negotiate or cancel with earnest money returned.
  • Section 8 (seller remedies): what happens if the buyer defaults — typically earnest money forfeited as liquidated damages.
  • Section 10a-10c (disclosures): seller property condition disclosure, lead-based paint disclosure (pre-1978), and Buyer Due Diligence Condition.
  • Section 24 (settlement deadline): the closing date. Missing it without a mutual extension can be a default.

The REPC updates periodically. Confirm your REALTOR is using the current version — UAR revises material clauses every few years.

Mandatory Utah Seller Disclosures

Utah sellers of residential property are required to disclose certain material facts about the property. The standard vehicle is the Seller Property Condition Disclosure form, which the UAR maintains:

  • Known material defects: roof, foundation, plumbing, electrical, HVAC, water intrusion history, prior insurance claims.
  • Lead-based paint (federal): for any home built before 1978, sellers must disclose known lead-based paint and provide the EPA pamphlet. Buyers get a 10-day testing window.
  • Meth contamination: if the seller knows the home has a history of meth contamination, Utah law requires disclosure. A property on Utah's meth decontamination list cannot be sold without decontamination documentation.
  • Radon awareness: not required by law, but Utah has one of the highest statewide rates of elevated indoor radon in the US. Utah DEQ recommends testing every home. See our Utah radon-testing guide.
  • HOA documents: if the home is in an HOA, the seller must provide CC&Rs, bylaws, and financials.

Sellers are not required to volunteer unknown defects — but they cannot knowingly conceal them. A seller who misrepresents the condition faces material contract and tort exposure. If a disclosure feels thin, request clarification in writing before your due-diligence deadline.

Earnest Money Mechanics in Utah

Earnest money is the buyer's up-front good-faith deposit, typically 1-3% of the purchase price on a Wasatch Front residential deal. Key mechanics:

  • The deposit is held by the title company or the brokerage trust account, not the seller.
  • If the buyer cancels within the REPC contingency windows (due diligence, financing, appraisal), earnest money is refunded.
  • If the buyer cancels outside those windows or defaults, earnest money is typically forfeited to the seller as liquidated damages (Section 8).
  • Earnest money is credited to the buyer at closing; it is not additional money, just an early portion of the down payment.

In competitive 2026 Wasatch Front markets, buyers sometimes offer "non-refundable earnest money" to strengthen an offer — meaning the deposit is released to the seller after a shortened due-diligence window. This is a strong offer tactic but a real risk if your inspection or financing goes sideways. Use it deliberately and only with your REALTOR's and lender's input.

Utah Agency Disclosure: Who Represents You?

Utah requires REALTORS to disclose their agency relationship on an Agency Disclosure form before writing an offer or substantive negotiation. The common relationships:

  • Buyer's agent: represents the buyer. Fiduciary duty runs to the buyer.
  • Seller's agent (listing agent): represents the seller. Fiduciary duty runs to the seller.
  • Limited agent (dual agency): same brokerage represents both buyer and seller. Agent cannot advocate for either side. Requires written consent from both parties.
  • Transaction broker: facilitates the transaction without representing either side.

If you are a first-time buyer, you almost always want a buyer's agent, not limited agency — you want someone whose fiduciary duty runs to you. Read the Agency Disclosure form carefully before signing, and ask the REALTOR which relationship they are proposing. Our Utah first-time buyer playbook walks through the full offer process.

Title, Property Rights, and Closing

Utah transactions close at a title company, which handles the deed transfer, title insurance, and funds disbursement. Key items at closing:

  • Title insurance: both a lender's policy (required by the lender) and an owner's policy (recommended) protect against title defects. The owner's policy is paid once and lasts as long as you own the home.
  • Deed: the document transferring ownership. Utah recognizes several deed forms (warranty, special warranty, quitclaim). A typical Utah residential sale uses a warranty deed from seller to buyer.
  • Vesting: how you take title — sole ownership, joint tenancy with right of survivorship, tenancy in common, or in a trust or LLC. Each has estate planning and tax implications; consult a Utah-licensed attorney if you are unsure.
  • Closing documents: expect to sign 40-80 pages at closing. Your REALTOR and title officer will walk you through.

Landlord-Tenant Law — URLTA

If you are buying a rental property, Utah's Residential Landlord-Tenant Act (URLTA) governs the relationship between you and your future tenants. Key points: 3-day pay-or-quit notice for nonpayment, 15-day notice before raising rent on a month-to-month, security deposits returned with itemized deductions within 30 days of move-out, and 24-hour entry notice for non-emergency inspections. Our Utah landlord guide covers URLTA in depth.

Zoning and Land Use

Utah zoning is set at the city level, not the state. Before buying a property with a plan — an ADU, an STR, a flip-to-duplex conversion — confirm the specific use is permitted by the city's current zoning. Zoning and short-term rental rules change; confirm current rules with the city's planning department before offering on anything. See our STR service page for city-by-city rules.

HOAs, CC&Rs, and Utah Common-Interest Communities

Properties in a planned community or condominium are subject to Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions (CC&Rs) plus HOA bylaws. These govern short-term rentals, exterior modifications, pets, parking, and more. The seller must provide these documents during due diligence — read them before you close. An HOA that bans your intended use is a deal-stopper.

Investor-Specific Considerations

If you are buying an investment property in Utah, add these items to your legal checklist:

  • Rental licensing: most Wasatch Front cities require a residential rental business license plus annual inspection on long-term rentals. Confirm with the city.
  • Short-term rental permits: Park City, Salt Lake City, and other Wasatch Front cities have specific STR permit regimes. Not every zone allows STR.
  • Entity structure: many investors hold rentals in an LLC. Utah LLC formation is straightforward; consult a Utah-licensed attorney and CPA on whether entity ownership makes sense for your situation.
  • Insurance: landlord policy and umbrella coverage. Your personal homeowner's policy will not cover a rental.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the REPC in Utah real estate?

The Real Estate Purchase Contract, or REPC, is the standard Utah buy-sell agreement promulgated by the Utah Association of REALTORS. It is the base contract virtually every Utah REALTOR uses on a residential purchase, and it defines earnest money mechanics, the due-diligence deadline, financing and appraisal deadlines, the settlement deadline, and seller disclosure obligations. The form updates periodically; make sure your REALTOR is using the current version. If you want a contract that is not the REPC, you should have a Utah-licensed real estate attorney draft it.

What does a Utah seller have to disclose?

Utah sellers of residential property typically complete the Seller Property Condition Disclosure form, covering known material defects in the roof, foundation, plumbing, electrical, HVAC, water intrusion history, prior insurance claims, and other items. Federal law also requires a lead-based paint disclosure for any home built before 1978. If the home has a known history of meth contamination, Utah law requires disclosure. Sellers are not required to volunteer unknown defects — but they cannot knowingly conceal them. A seller who misrepresents the condition faces material contract and tort exposure.

How does earnest money work in a Utah purchase?

Earnest money in Utah is the buyer's up-front deposit demonstrating serious intent, typically 1-3% of the purchase price. It is held by the title company or brokerage trust account, not the seller. If the buyer cancels within the REPC contingency deadlines (due-diligence, financing, appraisal), the earnest money is refunded. If the buyer cancels outside those deadlines or defaults on the contract, the earnest money is typically forfeited to the seller as liquidated damages. The exact mechanics are in Section 8 of the current REPC.

Do I need a radon test when buying a Utah home?

Utah has one of the highest statewide rates of elevated indoor radon in the US. The Utah Department of Environmental Quality recommends testing every home before purchase. Radon is not required by law to be tested, but it is almost always a smart due-diligence step, especially on a basement-finished Wasatch Front home. A radon test runs $150-$250 and takes 48-96 hours. Elevated readings are mitigable with a vent system that typically costs $1,500-$3,000. See our Utah radon-testing guide for full detail.

What is dual agency in Utah real estate?

Dual agency occurs when one real estate brokerage represents both the buyer and the seller in the same transaction. In Utah it is called a "limited agency" relationship, and it requires written consent from both parties on the Agency Disclosure form. A limited agent cannot advocate for either side; they facilitate the transaction neutrally. Many buyers prefer not to have limited agency because they lose a fiduciary advocate. If you have questions about the different agency relationships Utah allows, your REALTOR should walk through the Agency Disclosure form with you before writing an offer.
