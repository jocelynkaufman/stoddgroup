Rental Property Investing in Utah: How to Analyze Cash Flow and ROI

First-Time BuyersHouse HackingInvesting

Most first-time Utah investors fail at Utah rental property cash flow underwriting the same way: they subtract their mortgage payment from their rent and call the difference "cash flow." They miss vacancy, repairs, capital expenses, and management, and a year later the property is net-negative. This guide walks the full cash-flow and ROI worksheet the way we underwrite every investor deal at Stodd Group, applied to real 2026 numbers in Salt Lake City, Ogden, and Provo — so your projections match reality by month 24.

This article is scoped to the cash-flow math on a single buy-and-hold rental. For the asset-class choice (duplex vs. SFR vs. 5+ unit multifamily) see our multifamily vs single family Utah guide; for market-level analysis see top Utah areas to invest.

The 50% rule: operating expenses are roughly half of gross rent

The 50% rule says total non-mortgage operating expenses on a rental property run about 50% of gross rent over any multi-year hold. Not 5%. Not 20%. Fifty. It is a back-of-envelope filter, not a precise calculation, but it is a useful first test.

The 50% covers: property tax, insurance, property management, vacancy, repairs and maintenance, capital expenses (roof, HVAC, water heater, flooring over time), utilities that stay with the owner, HOA, landscaping, and periodic make-ready between tenants. On a $1,800/month rent, that is $900/month in operating expenses before you pay the mortgage. If your mortgage payment is $1,400, you are negative $500. If your mortgage payment is $800, you are positive $100.

In 2026 most Wasatch Front deals at 25% down come in slightly better than the 50% rule — Ogden and Davis-Weber single-families often run 42-47% operating expenses on a $1,800-$2,200 monthly rent. Salt Lake County and Utah County single-families often run worse because property values (and therefore property taxes and insurance premiums) outpace rents.

The 1% rule: where it works in Utah and where it doesn't

The 1% rule says a property "works" if monthly rent is at least 1% of purchase price. A $300,000 property rented for $3,000/month is a 1% deal.

In 2026 Utah, the 1% rule is effectively dead in Salt Lake County and Utah County on new purchases with institutional financing. A $450K SLC starter rarely rents above $2,400. In Ogden, Clearfield, and Roy you can still find individual deals at 0.6-0.7% that cash-flow because operating expenses and mortgages are smaller in absolute dollars, even though the ratio is well under 1%.

The practical bar today for a Utah single-family rental: 0.6-0.7% rent-to-price with a 25% down DSCR or conventional, in a market where the absolute rent covers absolute expenses plus a reserve. The 1% rule still works for private-money or seller-finance deals, for value-add BRRRR properties where ARV-to-rent improves after rehab, and for 4-plex and small multifamily in Ogden.

The Utah rental property cash flow worksheet, line by line

Every property we underwrite goes through this worksheet. Fill all 17 lines before you offer.

Revenue

  • Line 1 — Gross monthly rent (based on three rent comps in the same zip)
  • Line 2 — Other income (parking, laundry, pet fees). Usually $0 for SFR.
  • Line 3 — Gross potential income = 1 + 2
  • Line 4 — Vacancy allowance (5-8% in stable Wasatch Front markets, 8-10% in student-heavy Provo)
  • Line 5 — Effective gross income = 3 – 4

Operating expenses (not including mortgage)

  • Line 6 — Property tax (Utah county residential rate, note non-primary residence rate without the primary-residence exemption is higher)
  • Line 7 — Homeowners insurance (landlord policy, typically higher than owner-occupant)
  • Line 8 — Property management (8-10% of gross rent in 2026 Utah; $0 if self-managing but build in your hourly rate)
  • Line 9 — Repairs and routine maintenance (budget 5-8% of gross rent)
  • Line 10 — Capital expense reserves (5-8% of gross rent set aside for roof, HVAC, water heater, flooring)
  • Line 11 — Utilities paid by landlord (water/sewer/garbage on many Utah rentals)
  • Line 12 — HOA, landscaping, snow removal, pest control
  • Line 13 — Total operating expenses = 6 + 7 + 8 + 9 + 10 + 11 + 12
  • Line 14 — Net operating income (NOI) = 5 – 13

Debt service and cash flow

  • Line 15 — Mortgage PITI minus the taxes and insurance already in lines 6-7 (so just P&I)
  • Line 16 — Monthly cash flow = 14 – 15
  • Line 17 — Annual cash flow = 16 × 12

If line 17 is below $2,400 (equivalent to roughly $200/month) on a single-family rental, pass or re-negotiate. The deal is too thin to absorb a single bad surprise — a leaking roof or a 60-day vacancy will wipe the whole year.

Salt Lake City example: $450K Taylorsville single-family

Rate assumption: these worked examples use a mid-6% conventional rate and ~mid-7% DSCR rate. Late-2026 Freddie Mac PMMS has traded in the low-to-mid 7% conventional band, so at today's rates your conventional cash flow will be roughly $100–$150/month worse than each illustrative figure below. The thesis — SFR rentals in the Wasatch Front rarely cash-flow on 25% down conventional financing at today's rates — holds either way.

$450K purchase, 25% down ($112,500), 30-year conventional at mid-6% ($2,100/month P&I). Three-bed/two-bath single-family in Taylorsville. Rent comps at $2,350/month.

  • Gross rent: $2,350
  • Vacancy (6%): –$141 | Effective: $2,209
  • Property tax (non-primary): $320 | Insurance: $110 | PM (8%): $188
  • Repairs (5%): $118 | CapEx reserves (6%): $141 | Utilities + HOA: $45
  • Total opex: $922
  • NOI: $1,287/month | P&I: $2,100
  • Cash flow: approximately –$813/month

This is why SLC single-family rentals rarely underwrite at today's rates. Appreciation and loan-paydown can still make the deal attractive over a long hold, but monthly cash flow is negative at 25% down, mid-6% rates.

Ogden example: $340K East Central historic

$340K purchase, 25% down ($85,000), 30-year DSCR at mid-7% ($1,700/month P&I). Renovated 3-bed/1-bath East Central Victorian. Rent comps at $1,950/month.

  • Gross rent: $1,950
  • Vacancy (6%): –$117 | Effective: $1,833
  • Property tax (non-primary): $225 | Insurance: $95 | PM (8%): $156
  • Repairs (6%): $117 | CapEx reserves (7%): $136 | Utilities + landscape: $65
  • Total opex: $794
  • NOI: $1,039/month | P&I: $1,700
  • Cash flow: approximately –$661/month at mid-7% DSCR, or roughly $50-$150/month positive at 25% down conventional mid-6%.

Ogden single-family at today's rates is closer to break-even than Salt Lake County but still requires good financing (conventional vs DSCR matters here) to produce positive monthly cash flow. A duplex in the same Ogden zip typically flips this into solidly positive territory.

Provo example: $485K BYU-adjacent with legal ADU

$485K purchase, 25% down ($121,250), 30-year conventional at mid-6% ($2,265/month P&I). 4-bed main + 2-bed legal basement ADU. Main rents at $2,300, ADU rents at $1,250. Student-heavy market with higher vacancy.

  • Gross rent: $3,550
  • Vacancy (9% student-heavy): –$320 | Effective: $3,230
  • Property tax (non-primary): $360 | Insurance: $145 | PM (10% student-heavy): $355
  • Repairs (6%): $213 | CapEx reserves (7%): $249 | Utilities: $95
  • Total opex: $1,417
  • NOI: $1,813/month | P&I: $2,265
  • Cash flow: approximately –$452/month on conventional 25% down at mid-6%.

Provo's single-family math is tougher because high purchase prices and student-market vacancy stack against you. The right Provo plays are either houses with multiple separable rooms rented by-the-room near BYU (gross rent rises materially, though management load does too) or value-add BRRRR properties bought below market and refinanced on improved comps. See our BRRRR method page for the refi math.

Cash-on-cash ROI, cap rate, and when each matters

Three return metrics every Utah rental investor should track:

  • Cash-on-cash return = annual cash flow / total cash invested (down payment + closing costs + rehab). Measures what your capital is earning each year. Target 8%+ on a stabilized Wasatch Front rental in 2026; 10%+ on a BRRRR/value-add.
  • Cap rate = NOI / purchase price. Ignores financing, so it lets you compare properties regardless of debt structure. Wasatch Front stabilized small multifamily is trading at 5-7% cap rates in 2026; single-family rentals are typically closer to 4-5%.
  • Total ROI (cash flow + principal paydown + appreciation) — the honest number. Includes the mortgage principal you pay down each month and the appreciation the property earns. On a Utah SFR with negative cash flow but 4% appreciation, total ROI often lands 12-18% because the appreciation and loan paydown components are large.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you calculate cash flow on a rental property in Utah?

Utah rental property cash flow equals gross rent minus vacancy minus all operating expenses (property tax, insurance, property management, repairs, capital-expense reserves, utilities, HOA) minus mortgage principal and interest. Budget operating expenses at 40-50% of gross rent on a typical Wasatch Front single-family rental, not the 10-20% most spreadsheets guess at. Target at least $200/month positive after all those deductions.

What is the 1% rule in Utah real estate?

The 1% rule says monthly rent should be at least 1% of purchase price (a $300K property should rent for at least $3,000/month). In 2026 Utah, the 1% rule is effectively dead on Salt Lake County and Utah County single-family at market prices with institutional financing. Ogden, Clearfield, and Roy single-families can still clear 0.6-0.7% and cash-flow with 25% down. Small multifamily (2-4 unit) in Ogden sometimes still hits 1%.

What is a good cash-on-cash return for a rental in Utah?

A good cash-on-cash return for a stabilized Wasatch Front rental in 2026 is 8%+ annually on cash invested (down payment plus closing costs plus rehab). Value-add BRRRR properties and small multifamily can target 10-15%. Properties producing less than 5% cash-on-cash should be chosen primarily for appreciation and principal paydown, not cash flow.

What is the 50% rule for rental property?

The 50% rule says operating expenses (every expense except the mortgage — property tax, insurance, management, vacancy, repairs, capital expenses, utilities, HOA) run about 50% of gross rent over any multi-year hold. It is a back-of-envelope filter. In 2026 Wasatch Front deals, actual operating expenses typically run 42-50% in Ogden and Davis-Weber, and 46-55% in Salt Lake and Utah counties where property taxes and insurance climb faster than rents.

What is a good cap rate for Utah rental property?

Cap rates on stabilized Wasatch Front small multifamily (2-4 unit) were trading 5-7% in 2026; stabilized 5+ unit multifamily 5.5-7%. Single-family rentals typically trade at lower cap rates of 4-5% because owner-occupant resale demand compresses yields. A "good" cap rate depends on the asset class and risk profile — compare to similar properties in the same market, not across asset classes.
