Utah's 45% Primary Residential Exemption: The New Filing Rule Every Homebuyer Needs to Know (2027)

Buying First-Time Buyers

When you buy a home in Utah, you probably budget for the down payment, closing costs, inspections, and maybe a new couch. Almost nobody budgets for a paperwork mistake that can nearly double their property tax bill.

Starting January 1, 2027, that mistake gets a lot easier to make. Utah's primary residential exemption, the tax break that knocks 45% off the taxable value of the home you live in, no longer carries over automatically to a new owner. If you buy a home and don't file the right form with your county, you could be taxed on the home's full market value instead of the discounted value.

The fix is a short form and a deadline on your calendar. Here's what changed, who it affects, and how to make sure your first tax bill in your new home is the one you planned for.

This article is general information, not tax or legal advice. Property tax rules are administered county by county, so confirm your situation with your county assessor, and consult a Utah-licensed CPA or attorney for advice specific to you.

What the Primary Residential Exemption Actually Does

Utah taxes homes based on their assessed market value. But if a home is someone's primary residence, the state exempts 45% of that value. In practice, you're only taxed on 55% of your home's market value.

That's a meaningful break, and it's been around a long time. For years it worked quietly in the background. Most buyers never thought about it because, in most counties, a home that was a primary residence under the last owner was simply presumed to stay one.

According to the Utah State Tax Commission, a home qualifies as a primary residence when:

  • Someone lives there as their primary home for at least 183 consecutive days in a calendar year. That someone can be you, the owner, or a full-time tenant.
  • It's the only exemption your household claims in Utah. The state limits it to one per household (defined as a married couple and their dependent children).

Second homes and vacation cabins don't qualify. If you split time between a Salt Lake condo and a place in Park City, only one of them gets the discount.

What Changes on January 1, 2027

Under the updated rules, property owners must file Form TC-473A with their County Board of Equalization to receive or keep the exemption when any of the following apply:

  • A change in ownership. That includes buying a home, but also deed transfers, moving a home into a trust, and even name updates on title.
  • The property didn't qualify last year. For example, a home that was a vacation property or sat vacant, and is now someone's primary residence.
  • The county has reason to believe it no longer qualifies. If the Board of Equalization asks, you need to respond with the form.

The Tax Commission is direct about it: the exemption does not automatically transfer to new owners after a sale. Every change in ownership interest requires a new TC-473A.

That's the part that will catch people. The seller had the exemption. The listing may show last year's tax bill, which reflects the discounted value. Your lender may have escrowed based on that number. And none of that means you have the exemption.

The deadline: September 15

The Tax Commission lists September 15 as the deadline to submit the application to your county's Board of Equalization, and it says late applications can't be accepted. Utah property tax notices typically go out in late summer, with bills due in the fall, so September 15 is your window to get the exemption on that year's bill.

If you close late in the year, after the deadline has passed, ask your county assessor how they'll treat your purchase for the current year and the next. Counties handle timing and notices a little differently, and it's worth a five-minute phone call to know where you stand.

What Missing the Filing Can Cost

The Tax Commission says that if you don't file when required, the property may lose the exemption for that tax year and be taxed at 100% of its market value instead of 55%.

Here's the math in plain terms. Going from 55% of value to 100% of value means your taxable value jumps by roughly 82%. With the same local tax rate, your property tax bill goes up by about the same proportion.

Illustration only (not a real bill): If a home's property tax would be $2,500 with the exemption, the same home taxed at full market value would owe roughly $4,550. That's about $2,050 more for one year, from one missed form.

Your actual numbers depend on your home's assessed value and your local tax rate, which varies by city, school district, and special service districts. Your county assessor's website usually has a lookup tool showing a parcel's market value and whether the exemption is currently applied.

There's a second, slower-moving cost too. If your lender set up escrow based on the seller's exempt tax bill and you lose the exemption, your escrow will come up short. That usually shows up as an escrow shortage notice and a higher monthly payment the following year.

Who This Affects (It's More People Than You'd Think)

First-time buyers

If you're buying your first home, this is one more item for your post-closing checklist. You're the most likely group to assume taxes are "handled" through escrow. They're paid through escrow, but the exemption itself is your responsibility. Our guide for first-time buyers in Utah walks through the rest of the process, from pre-approval to keys.

Move-up buyers and anyone relocating within Utah

If you're selling one Utah home and buying another, remember the one-per-household limit. You'll file for the new home, and your old home will lose the exemption once it's no longer your primary residence (unless it becomes a long-term rental with a qualifying tenant; more on that below). If you're buying a home anywhere along the Wasatch Front, plan the filing into your move timeline the same way you'd plan utilities.

Anyone changing how title is held

This one surprises people. Moving your home into a living trust, adding a spouse to title, or correcting a name on the deed can all count as a change in ownership interest. If you're doing estate planning, ask your attorney to include the TC-473A filing as part of the process.

Landlords and house hackers

Long-term rentals can qualify for the exemption as long as a full-time tenant lives there as their primary home. Under the new rules, the Tax Commission says rental owners must submit a residential declaration signed by all property owners. The county assessor may ask for a copy of the current lease or the tax schedule showing rental income, though county officials are prohibited from contacting your tenants directly.

If you're house hacking a duplex or a home with a basement unit, you'll likely be dealing with both sides of this: your own unit as an owner-occupant and the rented unit as a residential rental. Short-term or nightly rentals generally won't meet the 183-day primary-residence test. Confirm how your county treats a mixed-use property before you rely on any specific tax number in your cash-flow math.

How to File Form TC-473A: A Simple Checklist

The form itself is short. The hard part is remembering it exists. Here's the sequence we suggest to clients:

  1. Before you close, look up the parcel. Use your county assessor's site to check the home's current market value and whether the exemption is applied. This tells you what the tax bill looks like with and without it.
  2. Budget for the unexempt number, just in case. When you're running affordability, it doesn't hurt to know the worst-case tax figure, especially if you're closing near the September deadline.
  3. Download the form. Form TC-473A is available on the Tax Commission's primary residential exemption page. Some counties use their own version or an online portal, so check your county's website first.
  4. File with your County Board of Equalization as soon as you've moved in. Don't wait for September. Filing early leaves time to fix anything the county flags.
  5. Keep a copy and a confirmation. Save the submitted form and any receipt or email from the county.
  6. Check your next valuation notice. When it arrives, confirm the exemption shows as applied. If it doesn't, call the assessor's office right away.

One reassuring note from the Tax Commission: counties can't ask for your purchase price or require information beyond what's on the state-approved forms. You're confirming how the home is used, not opening your finances.

A Note on New Construction

If you're building a home you plan to live in, the Tax Commission points owners to a different form, Form PT-24, rather than TC-473A. If you're buying new construction from a builder, ask your county which form applies to your situation and when to file it. The right answer can depend on where the home was in the construction process on January 1.

Questions to Raise Before and After Closing

Your agent, lender, and title company each touch a piece of this, but no single person "owns" the exemption filing except you. A few good questions to ask along the way:

  • To your lender: "Is my escrow estimate based on the exempt or unexempt tax amount?"
  • To your title or escrow officer: "How are property taxes being prorated at closing, and does that assume the exemption?"
  • To your county assessor: "I just purchased this parcel. What's the filing method and deadline for the residential exemption this year?"
  • To your CPA or attorney (if you're using a trust, LLC, or renting part of the home): "Does how I hold title change whether this property qualifies?"

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Utah's primary residential exemption?

Utah's primary residential exemption reduces the taxable value of your primary home by 45%, so you're only taxed on 55% of the home's assessed value. It's one of the biggest reasons Utah homeowner tax bills are lower than the sticker value suggests, and it applies only to the home you actually live in — not second homes, pure rentals, or vacant land.

Does the primary residential exemption transfer when I buy a home in Utah?

Starting January 1, 2027, no. Under the new rule, the exemption does not transfer automatically to a new owner. If you buy, inherit, or retitle a Utah home, you must file Form TC-473A with your county's Board of Equalization to claim it — even if the prior owner was already exempt.

What happens if I miss the September 15 deadline to file TC-473A?

If you miss the deadline, the exemption is not applied for that tax year and you'll owe property tax on 100% of the assessed value instead of 55%. On a typical Wasatch Front home, that difference can run several hundred to a few thousand dollars for a single year. You can still file for the following year, but you cannot retroactively recover the extra tax paid.

Who qualifies for Utah's 45% primary residential exemption?

Any Utah homeowner whose property is their primary residence — the home they actually occupy as their main dwelling. House hackers who live in one unit of a duplex or triplex typically qualify on their owner-occupied unit. Pure rentals, second homes, and short-term rentals the owner doesn't live in do not qualify. If title is held in a trust or LLC, confirm with your CPA or attorney.

How do I file Form TC-473A in Utah?

Download Form TC-473A from the Utah State Tax Commission or your county assessor's website, complete it with your parcel number and owner information, and submit it to your County Board of Equalization by September 15. Most Wasatch Front counties (Salt Lake, Utah, Davis, Weber) accept online submission; the smaller counties still take paper. Keep your filed copy with your closing documents.

The Takeaway

Utah's 45% primary residential exemption is still a valuable benefit for Utah homeowners. What's changing in 2027 is that it's no longer automatic for new owners. If you buy, inherit, or retitle a home, plan to file Form TC-473A with your County Board of Equalization, ideally right after you move in and no later than September 15.

Put it on your closing checklist next to "change the locks" and "transfer utilities." It's a few minutes of paperwork that protects a meaningful chunk of your annual housing cost.
