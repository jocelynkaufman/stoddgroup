House Flipping Opportunities in Salt Lake City, Ogden, and Provo: A Utah Investor's Guide

First-Time Buyers Investing Neighborhoods

Utah house flipping in 2026 looks different than it did at the 2022 peak. Price appreciation no longer carries marginal deals the way it did when Wasatch Front comps rose 15-20% a year; today a flip has to work on renovation uplift and clean buy-side underwriting. The flip markets that still pencil are Salt Lake City (east-bench tear-downs and 1950s ranchers), Ogden (East Central Victorians and foursquares), and Provo (older BYU-area single-family and infill lots). This guide runs the actual 70% rule math in each of those three Utah markets with 2026 cost and ARV assumptions, then covers how we'd structure financing on your first flip.

This is the flip-math article; for buy-and-hold cash-flow analysis see our Utah rental property cash flow guide, and for the broader market-analysis thesis see top areas to invest in Utah real estate.

The 70% rule applied to Utah house flipping

The 70% rule is the industry's standard back-of-the-envelope for whether a flip is worth pursuing. The formula:

Maximum Allowable Offer (MAO) = (After-Repair Value × 0.70) – Repair Costs

The 0.70 multiplier builds in your target profit, holding costs, selling costs (closing, realtor commission, title), and financing costs. It does not mean you cannot pay more; it means paying more erodes your margin. For first-time Utah flippers we recommend holding to 0.70. Experienced operators with consistent crews sometimes push to 0.72-0.75 in strong-comp pockets like Sugar House or the Avenues.

Three inputs you must underwrite correctly for Utah house flipping to actually work:

  • ARV (After-Repair Value): pull three solds in the past 90 days within half a mile, same bed/bath count, and renovated to the standard you intend to deliver. Use Salt Lake Board of Realtors MLS data, not Zillow Zestimates.
  • Repair costs: have a general contractor walk the property with you and give a line-by-line bid before you offer. Guessing repair costs is how new flippers lose their first deal.
  • Holding costs: include hard-money or DSCR interest, property tax, insurance, utilities, HOA, and permit fees for the full expected hold. A Utah flip holds 4-7 months on average.

Salt Lake City flip example: 1950s east-bench rambler

Consider a tired 1950s east-bench rambler in a neighborhood where renovated comps are closing at $775,000 (ARV). Repair scope is a full kitchen and both baths, refinished hardwoods, HVAC, electrical panel, roof, and basement finish — roughly $130,000 of work.

  • ARV: $775,000
  • MAO (70% rule): ($775,000 × 0.70) – $130,000 = $412,500
  • Gross spread (sale minus purchase, repairs, and 70% buffer): roughly $120,000-$135,000 after all costs.

On the east bench in 2026, that MAO is competitive with other offers but not a slam dunk. Tear-down lots in the Avenues or Sugar House push comps even higher; ARVs on renovated infill new-construction can hit $1.1M-$1.4M, which widens the MAO materially on the right lot.

Ogden flip example: East Central Victorian

An East Central Victorian in a neighborhood where fully renovated 3-bed/2-bath Victorians are closing at $475,000 (ARV). Scope is kitchen, both baths, mechanicals, foundation parging, new roof, sewer scope and partial line replacement — roughly $110,000 of work.

  • ARV: $475,000
  • MAO (70% rule): ($475,000 × 0.70) – $110,000 = $222,500
  • Gross spread after all costs: roughly $55,000-$75,000.

Ogden's math has thinner absolute dollars than SLC, but on a percentage-return basis the Ogden flip often beats the east-bench SLC flip because your capital exposure is smaller, your hold time is similar, and buyer demand for renovated Ogden historic stock remains strong. Pull a sewer scope on every pre-1960 Ogden property — lateral clay lines are the single biggest surprise cost here.

Provo flip example: older BYU-area single-family

An older 4-bed single-family near the BYU campus where renovated comps with legal basement apartments are hitting $625,000 (ARV). Scope includes a basement-apartment buildout to add a legal rental unit, plus kitchen, upstairs bath, and roof — roughly $140,000.

  • ARV: $625,000
  • MAO (70% rule): ($625,000 × 0.70) – $140,000 = $297,500
  • Gross spread after all costs: roughly $75,000-$95,000 if sold; materially better if held as a BYU-area long-term rental.

Provo's unique angle is the ADU-rental market around BYU. A legal, permitted basement apartment can add $40,000-$75,000 to ARV in the resale pool and is often the margin-maker. Confirm current ADU and rental licensing rules with Provo's planning department before the scope is locked — city rules on owner-occupancy requirements and unit size vary by zone and change every few years.

Typical renovation costs in Utah (2026)

Rough budget ranges we see on Wasatch Front flips in 2026:

  • Kitchen (mid-grade finish): $25,000-$55,000
  • Bathroom gut-and-remodel: $12,000-$25,000 per bath
  • Roof (asphalt comp, standard SFR): $12,000-$22,000
  • HVAC (replace furnace and AC): $9,000-$15,000
  • Electrical panel + sub-panel upgrade: $3,500-$6,500
  • Refinish hardwoods: $4,000-$8,000
  • Finish basement (plumbed): $35,000-$65,000
  • Legal ADU/basement apartment buildout: $75,000-$135,000 depending on existing framing and permits

Build a 10-15% contingency on top of these for every flip. Wasatch Front labor is short and lumber prices still swing more than they did pre-2020.

Financing a Utah house flip: hard money vs. DSCR

You rarely get a 30-year conventional loan on a flip. The two realistic options:

  • Hard money / private money: short-term (6-18 months), typically 10-12% interest plus 1-3 points, lends against ARV (often 85% of purchase + 100% of rehab, capped at 70-75% of ARV). Best for a classic buy-rehab-sell flip.
  • DSCR loan: 30-year mortgage that qualifies on the property's rental income instead of your W-2, typical rates 1-1.5 points above conventional, 20% down. Best when you plan to BRRRR (refinance and hold as a rental) rather than sell.

Common first-flip mistakes on Wasatch Front deals

Four mistakes we see on more than half of first-time Utah house flipping deals — none of them hard to avoid once you know to look for them:

  • ARV comps that are already stale. Any sold comp older than 90 days in a changing market is unreliable, and 2026's Wasatch Front has swung from rising to flat. Use three solds inside 90 days, within a half-mile, same bed/bath, renovated to your target standard.
  • GC bid that excludes site work and permits. Many first-time flippers get a bid on finishes and forget permit fees ($800-$3,500 per municipality), engineering, dumpster, and utility reconnects. Add 8-12% on top of any GC bid to cover the work the bid is not covering.
  • Underestimating sewer, foundation, and HVAC on older stock. Pre-1960 Ogden and east-bench Salt Lake City properties regularly need lateral sewer-line replacement ($5,500-$15,000), foundation parging or partial underpinning ($4,000-$18,000), and full HVAC swap. Pull a sewer scope and a structural walk-through before you offer on any pre-1960 property.
  • Not underwriting the carry cost of a soft comp market. If your ARV comes down 5-8% between offer and listing, your carry cost goes up every month the house sits. Build in a 60-90 day market exposure in your model, not a 7-day sell-through.

The way to avoid all four: have an experienced flip-oriented buyer's agent walk the deal with you before offering. We do this with investor clients regularly — the margin saved on one avoided mistake typically covers an entire year of agent relationship.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is house flipping profitable in Utah?

Yes, for operators who buy right. Utah house flipping in 2026 pencils in Salt Lake City east-bench, Ogden East Central, and Provo BYU-area neighborhoods with disciplined 70% rule underwriting and a general contractor's walk-through bid before you offer. Appreciation-only speculation (buying at the margin and hoping prices rise) is no longer profitable the way it was in 2021-2022.

How much profit do you make from house flipping in Utah?

Typical gross profit on Utah house flipping in 2026 ranges from $55,000 to $130,000+ per deal depending on ARV band and renovation scope. Ogden flips often clear $55,000-$80,000 at a lower capital exposure; Salt Lake City east-bench flips can clear $100,000-$150,000 but require more capital. Percentage returns (ROI on cash invested) are often similar across the three markets.

What is the 70% rule for house flipping?

The 70% rule says your maximum allowable offer (MAO) on a flip equals the after-repair value times 0.70, minus your repair costs. The 0.70 multiplier covers your profit, holding costs, selling costs (realtor commission, title, closing), and financing interest. New Utah flippers should hold strictly to 0.70; experienced operators sometimes stretch to 0.72-0.75 in strong-comp pockets.

How much does it cost to flip a house in Utah?

Full-scope Wasatch Front flips typically carry $90,000-$160,000 in renovation costs in 2026, with east-bench Salt Lake City runs trending higher and Ogden runs often lower on absolute scope. Add 10-15% contingency on top of the GC bid, and underwrite hard-money interest plus holding costs for a 4-7 month expected hold.

What areas in Utah are best for house flipping?

On the Wasatch Front, the best Utah house flipping markets in 2026 are Ogden East Central and 25th Street for historic Victorians, Salt Lake City east-bench and Sugar House for 1950s-60s ranchers and tear-down lots, and Provo BYU-area for older single-family with legal ADU potential. Secondary opportunities exist in Clearfield, Roy, Magna, and parts of Tooele depending on specific comps.
