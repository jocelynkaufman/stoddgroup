Top 10 Tips for First-Time Home Buyers in Utah: A Guide to House Hacking

First-Time BuyersHouse HackingInvesting

Buying your first home in Utah in 2026 looks very different than it did two years ago. Mortgage rates still sit well above the 3-4% range Utah families got used to in 2020-2021, inventory on the Wasatch Front has loosened compared to the 2022 peak, and down-payment-assistance programs through Utah Housing Corporation have expanded. The good news for anyone becoming a Utah first time home buyer: lenders, builders, and sellers are now willing to negotiate in ways they weren't in 2022-2023. These ten tips are the exact playbook we walk every first-time client through at Stodd Group — from credit prep to the closing table.

This guide is for the owner-occupant moving into a primary residence along the Wasatch Front: Salt Lake, Utah, Davis, Weber, Summit, Wasatch, Tooele, or Cache county. Our first-time buyer resource page has a parallel checklist; this article is the strategy behind each box.

1. Fix your credit six months before you want to buy

A 740+ FICO is the score mortgage underwriters reward most in 2026. The gap between a 740 and a 680 on a $450,000 Utah home with 5% down is often close to a full percentage point on the rate — roughly $230-$280 a month on a 30-year fixed payment. Over five years that is more than $14,000.

Three concrete moves in the six months before you apply:

  • Pull your free report at annualcreditreport.com and dispute anything that isn't yours.
  • Pay credit card balances down to below 10% of their limit by the statement date, not the due date — statement balances are what get reported.
  • Do not open or close any accounts, and do not finance a car or furniture. One new tradeline can drop your score 20-40 points.

2. Get pre-approved, not pre-qualified

These are different documents. A pre-qualification is a conversation; a pre-approval means your lender pulled your credit, verified your income with pay stubs or tax returns, and ran the file through automated underwriting. Listing agents on the Wasatch Front filter out pre-qualification letters in multiple-offer situations, so you want the pre-approval in hand before you tour your first home.

Ask the lender for a letter dated within the last 30 days. Request one letter at your true maximum approval for your records, plus a second letter matching each specific offer price. Sellers don't need to see your ceiling.

3. Tap Utah first time home buyer down payment assistance (UHC)

Utah Housing Corporation (UHC) runs several first-mortgage products paired with a second-mortgage down-payment-assistance loan — FirstHome, HomeAgain, Score 580, and No MI are the main ones. The DPA second covers up to 100% of your down payment and closing costs. It's a fully amortized second mortgage, not a grant, but the rate is reasonable and it can let you walk in with effectively zero cash down if you qualify.

Income caps apply and vary by county. Not every lender is a UHC-approved originator — ask directly, "Are you set up to originate UHC FirstHome?" before locking in. For the broader government-vs-conventional comparison, see our breakdown of primary residence loan products in Utah.

4. Pick the right neighborhood before the right house

The most expensive mistake a Utah first time home buyer can make is falling for a house in a neighborhood they'd leave in three years. A starter condo in Daybreak, a townhome in Lehi, and a bungalow in Sugar House are all reasonable first homes — they appreciate at different rates and attract different resale buyers.

Before you tour, rank what matters most to you: commute, school boundary (even without kids — schools drive resale), walkability, HOA rules, and future development. Our guides to most walkable Utah neighborhoods guide and up-and-coming Wasatch Front neighborhoods cover the trade-offs at different price points.

5. Write a clean offer in a flat market

In 2026 the Wasatch Front is more balanced than it has been since 2019. That means you can negotiate again — but a clean offer still wins. "Clean" doesn't mean waiving everything; it means removing friction the seller cares about.

  • Earnest money deposit: typical range is 1-3% of purchase price; 2% signals you're serious without catastrophic exposure.
  • Financing contingency: keep it. Pre-approval is not final underwriting approval.
  • Appraisal contingency: keep it unless you have the cash to cover a short appraisal.
  • Inspection period: 7-10 days is standard; shortening to 5 days is a modest concession that can win a house.
  • Close date: ask the listing agent what timeline the seller wants, then write to it. Matching their preferred close date is free and can beat a $5,000-higher offer with the wrong date.

6. Treat the inspection period as your second negotiation

The initial offer is the first negotiation; the inspection repair request is the second, and first-time buyers underuse it. A thorough home inspection on a typical Utah single-family home runs $400-600 and is the highest-ROI expense you'll incur before closing.

What you're looking for: active roof leaks, sewer line issues (a sewer scope is a $150-250 add-on, worth it on any pre-2000 home), HVAC approaching end of life, outdated electrical panels (Federal Pacific Stab-Lok and Zinsco are known safety hazards), radon test results above the EPA action level of 4 pCi/L, and foundation movement beyond cosmetic. Any of these is grounds to ask for a credit, a price reduction, or seller-funded repairs before closing. Walk away if the seller refuses on something structural and material.

7. Understand closing costs before you sign

Buyer closing costs in Utah typically land in a 2-4% range of the purchase price. On a $450,000 home that is $9,000-$18,000 on top of your down payment, so you want your lender walking you through a detailed Loan Estimate the week you go under contract.

Line items that are negotiable (the seller can pay some or all): owner's title insurance, transfer fees, HOA transfer fees, and most of the escrow prepaids. Line items that aren't: appraisal, credit report, underwriting, your portion of property-tax reserves, and your first-year homeowners insurance premium. Our breakdown on how to afford a house in the Salt Lake Valley runs the full math on a specific example.

8. Have a rate-lock strategy before you go under contract

You cannot lock a mortgage rate until you're under contract on a specific property. Once you are, you'll have a 30, 45, or 60-day lock window, and in 2026's rate environment the float-vs-lock call matters. If you believe rates are trending down in your lock window, a float-down option (available from some lenders at a small fee) is often worth it. If you believe they're trending up, lock the day you go under contract.

Our Salt Lake City mortgage rate shopping guide walks through comparing lender quotes line by line.

9. Budget three months of reserves after closing

Lenders check reserves at underwriting; they don't follow up after closing. You should. A reasonable target is three months of PITI (principal, interest, taxes, insurance) plus one HVAC service call sitting in savings the day you move in. Utah homes are hard on HVAC — hot summers, cold winters — and the first unexpected repair always comes faster than you think.

10. Work with an agent who closes Wasatch Front first-time buyers every month

A general residential agent can sell you a home. An agent who regularly closes with first-time buyers knows which lenders move fast, which listing agents accept clean offers over marginally higher ones, which neighborhoods appreciate steadiest, and which inspectors miss foundation issues. Frequently Asked Questions

How much do I need to put down as a first time home buyer in Utah?

Utah first time home buyers can put down as little as 0% using Utah Housing Corporation DPA stacked on an FHA first mortgage, 3.5% with FHA alone, or 3% with a conventional HomeReady or Home Possible loan. VA loans are 0% down for eligible veterans. Most Utah FTHB clients close with 3.5% to 5% down plus 2-4% in closing costs.

What credit score do I need to buy a house in Utah?

FHA accepts credit scores as low as 580 for 3.5% down (500-579 requires 10% down). Conventional loans generally start at 620, with the best pricing at 740+. UHC's Score 580 Loan is designed for buyers between 580 and 620. Shop lenders — different lenders apply different overlays on top of the base guidelines.

Is now a good time to buy a house in Utah?

The Wasatch Front market in 2026 is more balanced than it has been since 2019, with sellers negotiating on price, closing costs, and rate buydowns. If your job is stable, your credit is at least 680, and you plan to hold the home three or more years, the cost of waiting (rent paid, missed appreciation, missed principal paydown) usually exceeds the cost of buying at current rates. Waiting for the perfect rate often costs more than buying now and refinancing later.

What are closing costs for a first time home buyer in Utah?

Closing costs in Utah typically run 2-4% of purchase price for the buyer. On a $450,000 home that is $9,000-$18,000. The bill includes lender fees, title insurance, escrow prepaids, appraisal, inspection, and property-tax reserves. Many of these are negotiable with the seller, especially in a slower market.

What is the Utah Housing Corporation first time home buyer program?

Utah Housing Corporation (UHC) is the state housing finance agency. It offers FirstHome, HomeAgain, Score 580, and No MI loan programs that pair a competitive first mortgage with a second-mortgage DPA loan covering up to 100% of your down payment and closing costs. Income limits apply and vary by county, and you must use a UHC-approved lender to originate the loan.
